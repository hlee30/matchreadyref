01 / The principle

Position for the decision, not just the ball.

You cannot outrun a pass. The difference is learning to read the next challenge early enough to arrive at a clear angle.

“
Be where you need to be to see what you need to see.

Positioning principle quoted in the seminar

See

Find the contact

Know what happened between players—not just who fell or where the ball ended up.

Angle

Open the sightline

Move laterally to reveal the space between the attacker and defender.

Distance

Be credible

Stay close enough to judge the intensity of a challenge while avoiding passing lanes.

02 / Visual recognition

See the negative space.

“Negative space” is the space between opponents where a challenge takes place. If a player's body blocks that space, you may miss the point of contact.

An open angle reveals the challenge

Move your feet to keep the relevant contact visible as the players move.

Three questions

What should I identify?

  1. Point of contact: Where did contact occur (foot, ankle, shin, upper body)?
  2. Degree of force: How much force was used, and was it careless, reckless or excessive?
  3. Who initiated: Which player caused the contact, and what was each player doing?
Important: Not every contact is a foul. Clear sightlines help you confidently recognize both offences and fair challenges.
03 / Viewing angles

A better angle beats a shorter distance.

Standing directly behind two players can hide a foul even if you are only a few yards away. Small lateral adjustments can make a major difference.

✕ Blocked line of sight

One player blocks the other. You may see the fall but miss how the challenge happened.

✓ Open line of sight

The same incident becomes easier to assess when you can see between the players.

Field habit: Instead of automatically running straight toward the ball, ask which side of the challenge gives you the best view. Your distance still needs to be credible.
04 / Anticipation

Read the pressure. Move before the challenge.

Pressure on the player in possession is a useful cue for deciding when to scan, when to adjust your position, and when to be ready for immediate contact.

SCAN & ANTICIPATE

Low pressure

The player has time and space to lift their head and look for a pass. You should scan too: where could the ball go, and where could your next decision happen?

  • Look beyond the ball carrier.
  • Identify likely receivers and defenders.
  • Begin shaping your next viewing angle.
05 / Field movement

Use the central corridor to transition—not to live in.

The seminar described a roughly 20-yard-wide central control path between penalty areas. It is often congested and can obstruct passing lanes. Move through it when necessary rather than remaining there.

Positioning concept · simplified field map

Illustrative zones, not a prescribed referee route. Actual positioning follows play.

Three practical rules

  • Don't camp in the middle: central space is often occupied by the defending team and passing lanes.
  • Don't stay on a rigid diagonal: enter the AR's side of the field when the next decision demands a better angle.
  • Don't abandon transition readiness: avoid moving so wide that you cannot recover when possession changes.
Assistant referee blind spot: an AR tracking the offside line may not clearly see a nearby challenge. The center referee still needs to seek a credible view of contact.
06 / Flow of the match

Recognize the four phases of play.

A transition can last only a second or two. Understanding the direction of play allows you to reposition before the next attack develops.

PHASE 01
Attacking

The team has possession and progresses forward.

PHASE 02
Attack → Defense

Possession changes; the former attacker reorganizes.

PHASE 03
Defending

The team protects space and challenges for possession.

PHASE 04
Defense → Attack

The ball is regained and a new attack begins.

What about unpredictable youth games? The presenter’s advice was to return to the same fundamentals: recognize the patterns each team actually uses, look for negative space, and prepare for the next pressured challenge—even when play is mostly long clearances.
07 / Energy and teamwork

Save energy where you can. Spend it where it matters.

In multi-match tournaments, effective positioning is more sustainable when you combine anticipation, fitness and help from your assistant referees.

Prepare

Scan in quieter moments

When the ball is under low pressure, read passing options and prepare your next move rather than running without a purpose.

Accelerate

Arrive for the key challenge

Prioritize credible positioning when pressure increases and a consequential decision becomes likely.

Collaborate

Use the whole crew

Maintain eye contact, agree on responsibilities pregame, and consult when another official has a clearer view.

The closing speaker also referred to A, B and C field areas: B as a scanning/energy-management area and A/C as areas requiring more active positioning. Their exact boundaries were not defined in the supplied transcript.

Set three specific goals before kickoff. After the game, record one example of what worked and one change for next time.

Appendix / Event operations

What the tournament organizers said.

These are instructions reported during the seminar. They are not universal competition rules and should be verified against the regulations for each match.

AreaInstructions shared at this event

Earlier briefingTwo 35-minute halves, eight-second goalkeeper possession, event-specific substitutions and injury-clearance requirements were discussed.
Law versus local procedure: The earlier briefing described allowing substitutes to enter before the outgoing player had fully left. Under standard IFAB Law 3, the substitute enters only after the replaced player has left and after the referee's signal. Treat the briefing as an event instruction to clarify with organizers, not a general Law 3 rule. The event also stated that certain new time-limit protocols were not being applied.
